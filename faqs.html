Lithium battery recycling in Australia

Facts, regulations, costs, and ESG outcomes — what every business and individual should know about lithium battery recycling in Australia.

The Australian landscape

Scale, regulation and current shortfalls

How much lithium battery waste does Australia produce?
Australia currently has around 5,000 tonnes of small handheld lithium batteries requiring recycling annually, with only approximately 15% currently being recycled. Total end-of-life battery volumes across all types reached around 198,000 tonnes in 2024, and are projected to exceed 744,000 tonnes by 2050 as EV uptake accelerates. The Australian battery recycling sector already contributes $2.1 billion to the economy annually, with significant growth forecast as lithium-ion streams grow.
What regulations govern lithium battery disposal in Australia?
Australia's National Waste Policy Action Plan set a goal to restrict lithium-ion batteries and e-waste from landfill from 2024. The federal government committed $23 million in 2024–25 to circular economy policy and legislative functions, and launched a $532 million National Battery Strategy in 2024 to bolster domestic recycling infrastructure and R&D. The B-Cycle scheme provides a national voluntary product stewardship framework for portable batteries, with collection points across the country. Additional state-level regulations — such as NSW rules on e-bike and e-scooter battery disposal — are being introduced as EV-related fires in waste streams increase.
Are lithium batteries classified as hazardous waste in Australia?
Lithium batteries are not universally classified as hazardous/regulated waste as of 2026, but they are subject to special handling requirements due to fire and thermal runaway risks. All batteries, especially critically damaged, swollen, or leaking batteries are treated as dangerous goods. Businesses that generate lithium battery waste in commercial quantities have a duty of care under state environmental legislation to ensure safe disposal — simply placing them in general waste is non-compliant in most jurisdictions and creates significant fire risk in waste and recycling facilities.
What happens to lithium batteries that go into general waste in Australia?
Lithium batteries in general waste are a leading cause of fires in Australian waste and recycling facilities. When crushed or punctured in compaction trucks or sorting machinery, they can ignite and are extremely difficult to extinguish. Beyond the safety risk, batteries that reach landfill release toxic metals — including cobalt, nickel, and lithium — into soil and groundwater. This represents both an environmental liability and a wasted economic resource.
What is the cost of recycling lithium batteries in Australia?
Recycling costs in Australia vary widely. Small consumer lithium batteries can cost up to $20/kg to recycle through standard commercial streams, with most NCM and LFP batteries priced around $1–$8/kg. By comparison, Chinese processors are paying the equivalent of $1,500–$5,300 AUD per tonne for lithium battery scrap, reflecting a more mature market. Sustainable Lithium Cells Australia operates a second-life model that extracts more value from spent batteries before recycling — which allows us to offer free or discounted collection to many QLD businesses that commercial recyclers would charge.
Why is so little lithium battery waste recycled in Australia compared to other countries?
Australia lacks the scale of domestic processing infrastructure that China and the EU have built. Approximately 90,000 tonnes of lithium batteries are recycled annually in China, with dedicated facilities operating at scale. In Australia, most collected material is currently shredded into "black mass" and exported overseas for further processing, meaning Australia captures only a fraction of the recoverable value. The EU has recently mandated minimum recycled content in new batteries — which will increasingly drive demand for domestically processed material and investment in local capability.
ESG & business outcomes

Why recycling responsibly matters for your business

How does battery recycling contribute to ESG reporting?
Proper lithium battery recycling directly supports Scope 3 emissions reduction — the supply chain and waste emissions that are increasingly scrutinised in ESG disclosures. Producing battery-grade lithium and cobalt from recycled sources can require up to 96% less raw material than mining new resources (ABRI, 2026), translating directly into avoided upstream emissions. Can businesses claim carbon credits or offsets for battery recycling?
Australia's Safeguard Mechanism and voluntary carbon markets recognise avoided emissions from circular economy activities. While direct carbon credit generation from battery recycling is still an evolving area under the Australian Carbon Credit Unit (ACCU) scheme, the certified CO₂ reporting we provide can be applied to internal carbon accounting, supply chain emissions disclosures, and voluntary offset claims. As standards tighten — particularly with the introduction of mandatory climate disclosures for large Australian businesses — documented recycling outcomes will become increasingly valuable.
What is product stewardship and do businesses have obligations?
Product stewardship is the principle that manufacturers, importers, and retailers share responsibility for the end-of-life management of their products. Australia's Product Stewardship Act 2011 provides the legislative basis, and the B-Cycle scheme is the primary voluntary framework for portable batteries. For large-format batteries — EV packs, BESS units — there is no mandatory scheme yet, but regulatory direction is clearly towards greater producer responsibility. Businesses that proactively engage responsible recyclers now are better positioned ahead of mandatory requirements, and can demonstrate ESG leadership in the interim.

Second-life & circular economy

Understanding second-life batteries

What is a second-life battery?
A second-life battery is one that has been retired from its original application — typically an EV or solar storage system — but still contains cells with sufficient capacity for use in a less demanding application. Rather than being immediately shredded, viable cells are extracted, tested, and rebuilt into new battery packs for off-grid solar, portable power, marine, or other uses. This extends the effective lifespan of the underlying cells and defers the emissions cost of producing new ones.
How much CO₂ is saved by reusing cells rather than producing new ones?
The greenhouse gas cost of producing a lithium-ion cell varies by country of manufacture and chemistry, but ranges from approximately 61–106 kg CO₂-equivalent per kWh of battery capacity. Sustainable Lithium Cells Australia calculates that our second-life program saves a minimum of 100 tonnes of CO₂ per year compared to equivalent new cell production. Across Australia, the cumulative material recycling potential for end-of-life lithium batteries is estimated at between 2,481 and 4,471 kilotonnes by 2050, with an estimated economic value of USD 23–44 billion.
What is "black mass" and why does it matter?
Black mass is the mixed metal powder produced when lithium batteries are shredded. It contains recoverable lithium, cobalt, nickel, manganese, and graphite. Currently, most Australian black mass is exported to Asia for hydrometallurgical processing, meaning Australia captures little of the downstream value. Investment in domestic black mass processing is a key focus of Australia's National Battery Strategy, and is expected to grow significantly as end-of-life volumes increase. Our pre-processing and disassembly work increases the value of material sent to downstream recyclers, improving overall recovery rates.
